### The thing most founders get backwards

**Decision 1 feels small and is nearly irreversible. Decision 2 feels large and is
almost free to change.** Naming architecture locks in the moment Chapter 002 ships —
changing it afterwards orphans everything published under 001. Meanwhile the assortment
carries zero inventory, so adding or dropping an article later costs one sample and one
shoot. Spend your deliberation accordingly.

# DECISION 1 — COLLECTION ARCHITECTURE

**`CHAPTER 001 — ASCENSION` — separating the permanent number from the changeable theme.**

### Why it matters
It sets the naming convention for every future release, and naming architecture is
load-bearing: it drives article codes, label and packaging typography, URL and
collection structure, email subject conventions, and — most importantly — *where brand
equity accumulates over years.* Equity either builds in a word six competitors already
use, or in a numbering system only we own.

### Recommendation
**Adopt it.** The chapter number is the permanent asset; the theme changes each release.
"ASCENSION 001" remains valid everyday shorthand, so nothing you have already grown
attached to is lost.

### What I modelled / assumed
- A cadence of 3–4 chapters per year, each open 6–10 weeks `[A]`
- That you want theme flexibility beyond 001 — **your clarification has now confirmed
  this, which effectively decides the question**
- That at least six active gym-apparel brands trade on Ascension/Ascend `[V, 2026-08-23]`
- That the chapter number can carry graphic weight on its own — a Phase 02/03 design
  assumption, unproven until the identity work is done

### What changes if you choose otherwise
Keeping `ASCENSION 001 / 002 / 003` means the brand is permanently themed on that word.
Three consequences: you compete for search visibility against six incumbents forever;
the number stops meaning "how far we have come" and starts meaning "how many ascension
collections we have made"; and by Chapter 003 the theme is exhausted, at which point
renaming costs you the coherence of the entire back catalogue. **This option also
directly contradicts your own instruction not to lock future collections into the
word.**

### Confidence — **HIGH**
The strongest recommendation of the six. It costs nothing, preserves your original
instinct, satisfies a constraint you have already stated, and removes a compounding
liability. I would need a reason I have not thought of to be talked out of it.

### Reversibility — **Cheap now. Very difficult after Chapter 002.**
Free to change today. Once a second chapter ships under one scheme, switching orphans
the first — the archive stops reading as a sequence, which is the entire point of the
system. **Treat this as effectively permanent from the moment Chapter 002 is
announced.**

# DECISION 2 — FIRST ASSORTMENT

**Heavyweight tee · hoodie · long-sleeve · embroidered cap. Sweatpants deferred to
Chapter 002 pending a physical tonal-match test.**

### Why it matters
It sets the sample budget, the photography scope, the price ladder, and — critically —
whether average order value can clear the CAC ceiling. It is the decision that most
directly determines whether paid acquisition is ever affordable.

### Recommendation
**Adopt the four.** Keep sweatpants out of Chapter 001.

### What I modelled / assumed
- Sample cost of roughly €25–45 per unit, doubled for variance testing → **8+ samples
  minimum** `[A]`
- **That embroidered caps are available at acceptable quality from the chosen EU
  provider — this is unverified and it matters**, because the cap carries the AOV
  argument `[A]`
- An autumn launch, which favours heavyweight and disfavours tanks and shorts
- That a two-unit basket is required to make acquisition economics work (§15, Rule 2)
- That tonal black mismatch between hoodie and joggers from different blank suppliers is
  the most common way a POD "set" reveals itself `[A — industry pattern, not measured]`

### What changes if you choose otherwise
**Add sweatpants now:** +2 samples and real upside — a hoodie-and-jogger set is the
strongest AOV instrument available to us. But the failure mode damages *two* products
rather than one: a black that does not match in daylight undermines the hoodie as well
as the joggers. This is a "yes, once a sample proves it" rather than a "no".

**Cut to tee + hoodie only:** cheaper and faster, but you lose the basket-builder, AOV
falls toward single-unit, and the CAC ceiling tightens from ~€30 to ~€16 — which makes
paid acquisition structurally unaffordable rather than merely difficult.

**Add more articles:** photography cost scales roughly linearly while attention does
not. The binding constraint is what we can photograph properly, not what we can afford
to hold.

### Confidence — **MEDIUM-HIGH**
High on the shape (a hero, a margin anchor, a season piece, a basket-builder). Medium on
the cap specifically, because its availability and embroidery quality are unverified.
If Phase 04 shows poor cap options, the *role* still needs filling — a beanie is the
obvious substitute for an autumn launch.

### Reversibility — **HIGH. The cheapest decision on this list to change.**
Zero inventory means adding an article later costs one sample plus one shoot. Removing
one costs nothing. Do not agonise here — the cost of being wrong is a few hundred euro
and a fortnight.

# DECISION 3 — NO-DISCOUNT POLICY

**Adopt the policy. Publish the practice — but do not yet publish a permanent historical
claim.**

### Why it matters
This is simultaneously a margin requirement and a positioning pillar. At a 2.8× floor
there is no room in the price for a discount, so the policy is not merely aesthetic —
the unit economics depend on it.

### Recommendation
Adopt it, and state it plainly in present tense: *"We don't discount. The price is the
price."* **Do not yet claim "we have never held a sale" or "we never will"** — earn that
claim over twelve months, then it becomes an asset worth far more than the sentence
costs.

### What I modelled / assumed
Modelled a 20% code against the tee, at 21% VAT `[A]`:

| | Full price | 20% off |
|---|---|---|
| Retail, VAT incl. | €65.00 | €52.00 |
| Net revenue after VAT | €53.72 | €42.98 |
| Landed cost | −€23.00 | −€23.00 |
| Payment fees | −€1.88 | −€1.55 |
| Returns allowance @8% | −€4.30 | −€3.44 |
| **Contribution** | **€24.54** | **€14.99** |
| **CAC ceiling at 15% net margin** | **≈€16** | **≈€8.50** |

**A 20% discount removes 39% of unit contribution and roughly halves what you can afford
to pay for a customer.** Assumed also that customers who buy once on discount expect it
again — an industry pattern rather than a measured figure.

### What changes if you choose otherwise
You gain a conversion lever and the ability to participate in Black Friday. You lose
about two-fifths of contribution on every discounted order, you train the customer to
wait for the next code, and differentiation pillar 4 disappears. There is a second-order
effect worth naming: to absorb routine discounting you would have to set the ladder
higher — the €65 tee becomes an €80 tee permanently "20% off", which is precisely the
price-as-fiction practice the brand is positioned against.

### Confidence — **HIGH on the economics · MEDIUM-HIGH on publishing it**
The math is not really arguable at these margins. Publishing is a genuine bet: it makes
the commitment harder to abandon, which is the point, but it also removes a lever in a
real emergency. The split recommendation above is how I would take the bet without
overspending it.

### Reversibility — **The policy is reversible. The published claim is not.**
You can quietly stop discounting or quietly start. But "we have never held a sale" is a
one-time, non-renewable asset: spend it once and it is gone permanently. Hence
recommending you publish the practice now and reserve the historical claim until it is
true and provable.

# DECISION 4 — BUDGET

**€2,500, spent in sequence: samples → photography → platform → paid media.**

### Why it matters
It determines photography quality, which is the single largest driver of premium
perception and therefore of whether a €65 price is legible at all. It also determines
how many blanks you can physically test before committing.

### Recommendation
**€2,500 if it is available and comfortable to risk.** The sequence matters more than
the total — money spent on paid media before a creative is proven is the standard way
this category loses five figures.

### What I modelled / assumed
- A half-day studio, one model and one assistant at roughly €800–1,200 `[A — EU day
  rates vary enormously by city; this is the weakest assumption in the brief]`
- That **you can art-direct the shoot yourself** using the Phase 03 brief `[A]`
- No videographer, and no paid campaign film at this tier
- 12–15 samples including doubles and wash testing
- That fixed monthly running costs land at €80–130 `[A]`

### What changes if you choose otherwise
**€500:** genuinely viable — this is POD, the business does not require capital to
exist. Photography becomes the binding constraint, so the brand looks like what it is
paying for. Expect a slower ramp and a higher risk of reading as POD.

**€5,000–€10,000:** diminishing returns *pre-launch*, because you would be spending
before knowing which product sells. The right home for that money is Chapter 002 and the
first bulk run of a proven SKU. Spending it now buys confidence, not information.

### Confidence — **MEDIUM**
The lowest-confidence recommendation of the six, for an honest reason: **I do not know
your available capital, your risk tolerance, or whether you can shoot and art-direct
yourself.** All three change the answer. If capital is tight, start at €500 — the plan
degrades gracefully rather than breaking.

### Reversibility — **HIGH. This is a sequence, not a commitment.**
Start at €500 and add as evidence arrives. The only irreversible portion is money
already spent on photography that turns out unusable — which is exactly why photography
comes after samples pass the gate, never before.

# DECISION 5 — LAUNCH WINDOW

**Early-to-mid November 2026. Silent through Black Friday week.**

### Why it matters
It sets every upstream deadline — clearance, identity, samples, shoot, build — and it
determines how much competitive noise the first chapter opens into.

### Recommendation
**Early-to-mid November, then deliberate silence through Black Friday.** One line if
anything at all: *"We don't discount."* A no-discount brand launching *into* the loudest
discount week would be drowned out; one that visibly refuses to participate is
memorable and costs nothing.

### What I modelled / assumed
- A 90-day path from approval in late August `[A]`
- POD sample shipping of 1–2 weeks `[A]`
- **No slippage, and — the fragile assumption — no failed sample requiring a second
  round.** A failed gate adds two to three weeks and pushes November to December `[A]`
- That heavyweight product sells better into autumn than into late spring

### What changes if you choose otherwise
**January:** hits the fitness-resolution peak — the highest-intent moment of the year —
and buys 8 more weeks for samples and content. But it is also the most competitive and
expensive window, every competitor is shouting, and your validated samples sit unlaunched
for two months. **This is the closest call in the brief.** It is defensible and I would
not argue hard against it.

**During Black Friday:** drowned out, and it contradicts Decision 3 in the same week you
establish it.

**December:** gift-season traffic, but you are an unknown brand competing against
established gifting, and delivery-date anxiety works against a POD lead time.

### Confidence — **MEDIUM**
The reasoning holds — a new brand benefits more from quiet than from volume — but this
is a judgement call about attention, not an arithmetic result. Reasonable people would
choose January.

### Reversibility — **Fully reversible until you announce a date. Then not.**
Which produces an operating rule worth adopting regardless of what you choose:

> **Never announce a launch date before samples have passed the gate.**

Moving a date you have given a waitlist costs credibility with the exact people you most
need. Until then, the date is yours to move freely.

# DECISION 6 — COUNTRY OF ESTABLISHMENT

**This is a fact I need from you, not a recommendation I can make.**

### Why it matters
More than it appears. It sets the VAT rate (≈19–25% across the EU, a several-point swing
in contribution on every unit), whether and when OSS registration applies, your returns
and consumer-law obligations, and the wording of your policies.

There is also a mechanism with a material cost implication: **whether you are
VAT-registered changes your input cost, not just your output price.** A VAT-registered
merchant with a valid VAT ID can generally treat intra-EU supply from the POD provider
as reclaimable or zero-rated; an unregistered one pays VAT on fulfilment and cannot
reclaim it. On a €17 fulfilment cost that is a swing of roughly €3.40 per tee —
which moves the 2.8× floor and therefore the retail price.
`[A — mechanism requires confirmation with an accountant and with the provider's own
VAT policy. Do not act on this paragraph without that confirmation.]`

### What I modelled / assumed
- **EU-established, 21% VAT** throughout the financial model
- Implicitly, **that you are or will be VAT-registered**, and that fulfilment costs are
  net of reclaimable VAT. If that is wrong, landed costs rise and the ladder moves up
- Distance selling below the €10,000 threshold at launch, so home-country VAT applies
  initially `[A]`

### What changes if you choose otherwise
**A different EU country:** the VAT rate shifts and contribution moves a few points in
either direction. The strategy is unaffected; only the numbers move.

**Non-EU establishment — UK, for example:** materially more complex. EU sales become
exports with IOSS considerations and customs handling, and returns handling gets harder.
**Differentiation pillar 5 — European speed — survives only if fulfilment remains inside
the EU.** It is still workable, but it stops being simple, and the compliance section
would need rewriting rather than adjusting.

### Confidence — **N/A**
I have no basis to guess and will not model a second scenario speculatively. Tell me the
country and I will re-derive §15 against the real rate.

### Reversibility — **The country is a fact, not a choice. The structure around it is
moderately reversible** — registration and OSS decisions can be changed with accountant
time and cost, but not for free, and not retroactively for VAT already handled wrongly.

# THE GLOBAL BRAND PRINCIPLE — LOCKED AT APPROVAL

Added by the founder at approval, and it governs everything above it.

| | |
|---|---|
| **BRAND MARKET** | **GLOBAL.** Designed for an international audience from the foundation. English-first. Not culturally dependent on France or Europe |
| **LEGAL ESTABLISHMENT** | **FRANCE.** The jurisdiction for VAT, tax, consumer law and compliance modelling only |

**France is where the company pays tax. It is not who the brand is for.** GYMREIGN is
never positioned as French gymwear, French fitness clothing or European-only apparel.

### The principle that makes this affordable

> **Global brand design costs nothing extra. Global operational selling is expensive.**

Identity, naming, language, sizing conventions and storytelling cost the same to make
globally legible as they would to make parochial — so do that now. Each market opened
operationally carries its own tax registration, consumer law, labelling and
extended-producer-responsibility surface — so sequence that.

```
GLOBAL BRAND FOUNDATION  →  CONTROLLED INITIAL MARKET  →  EXPANSION  →  GLOBAL
    free, do it now           compliance-bounded          per-market     distribution
```

The compliance surface is an argument for phased **market opening**. It is never an
argument for parochial **brand design**.

### What changed in the strategy as a result

Differentiation pillar 5 was "European speed"; it is now **fulfilment proximity** — we
produce close to the customer, in whichever market the customer is in. The general form
is stronger, because it names the real advantage: **POD gives distributed manufacturing
without distributed capital.** A bulk competitor needs warehouses in three regions and
the money to stock them. We need a provider with facilities in three regions. That is
what makes global-from-day-one financially possible now rather than in year three.

POD provider selection changed accordingly: **do not choose a provider because it is
convenient for France.** EU, UK and US fulfilment, cross-region colour consistency, and
scalability are now selection criteria, not preferences. Cross-region consistency is the
one that cannot be checked from a dashboard — it requires ordering the same SKU from two
regions and comparing them physically.

### The one risk, stated plainly

A brand that tries to speak to everyone speaks to no one. Global relevance is not
achieved by removing specificity until nothing remains — that produces the placeless,
beige brand that is the standing failure mode of "international" positioning. It comes
from being **aesthetically specific and nationally unbounded.** Discipline, architecture,
restraint and earned progress are legible in every market on the list. They are specific
ideas. They are simply not French ones, or American ones.

**Phase 02 will be judged against exactly this.**
